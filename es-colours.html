Elizabeth's Colours

From Chapter Two of Dreamers Are Welcome

Once, Elizabeth had been something of a painter. She had started as a convalescent after her first bout of illness, filling up canvases with the ghostly shapes of colour that resembled rainbows hidden in fog. “Bold work, for a woman,” a friend’s second cousin, an art critic, remarked. “But quite emotionless.” She had stood, a tower beside this moustachioed martello, his striped kipper tie parting the livid spots on his shirt, and agreed with a delight he had not expected. “That’s what comes after a surfeit.” Of course, when the emotion returned so did her illness but, on days when she was unable to speak and her limbs ossified so she felt unable to get out of bed, even to use the lavatory, it was the thought of the neglected easel that made her break every bone in her body to stand.

She painted and from the tender strokes of her brush came, somehow, her monstrosities, all blackened eyes or eyes full of light in gorged heads and pavements chalky from the impact of drilling rain and the lights of shopfronts glinting through fog in every colour but gold. Thanks to a contact more encouraging than the first of the critics she had a small exhibition on the Kings Road. The venue was tearooms, a dainty place where elderly ladies met their nieces during the day. But it was infamous as the establishment where the same young girls rendezvoused with bohemians with curled moustaches and electric blue suits on insouciant school nights. The effeminate owner insisted the clientele all dug her work, but their money was ‘for food and drinks in restaurants where they might breathe the same air as Terence and the Shrimp, the darlings.’

In the years to come Leopold would encourage her and so on she would paint, her brushstrokes less tender, her canvases a tickertape of aggressive loops that had the same origin, a pool of gold that had burned away the mists and the shadows to reveal a fractured spectrum coagulating into a paste. It had not been marriage that had rescued her from a land where gold was exiled, it was all down to the tree. “The tree,” she whispered, smiling, and she thought of the tree undressing in front of her and how, on that long night a lifetime ago, everything had changed.

She was painting with gold now, a throne for the riot of stripes that was her interpretation of Leopold, and what a marvellous feeling it was, to succumb to inspiration that seemed somehow blind, to transfer the composition of the poster from her head on to the card. She had forgotten that power. She had forgotten so much, and suddenly old canvases she had painted flashed through her mind with a richness of detail that was staggering. What it is to be young and to make things happen, she thought. To walk with confidence down the street, the future fractured before you, a thousand turnings to take, a thousand possibilities that might come true. She had walked like that, striding along a sun-bleached path, aware of the allowance that was to be hers and with it the freedom to go to art school, aware too of the builders upon new roofs admiring her confident, elegant beauty. And the memory of this sketched the idea of a painting in her head, a complicated perspective of dusty lines combatting a shock of yellow sun, and the figure that danced where the dust met the shine was a collection of curves that were flames, and the pavement must be the colour of flint.

And when I am done painting the poster advertising Leopold’s debutante ball, I must paint that day almost sixty years old, and then another, and another, until all my life is recorded in every colour under the sun. But why stop at that? Why not claim a need for a retrospective of work, here in her adopted city of Amsterdam and, to prove her life was not yet over, exhibit new paintings, a consideration of her past, yes, but also an interpretation of Leopold’s forthcoming party? For she still had a future.
