The Diplomat’s Wife

From Chapter Two of Dreamers Are Welcome

But Elizabeth was lost in a reverie and she chattered on, sometimes to herself and sometimes to the cat. “My brain frosts at the thought!” she said and she remembered the years of hosting banquets for the master of decorum that was her handsome, dedicated but anxious husband, and of how it was rare to find some other diplomat’s wife with a flavour of mischief to be spotted in her colourful conversation. Candy Brompton was her favourite, and for most of the 70s and 80s she was an attendee at the annual ball they held in Curacao. “Candy Brompton,” she said to the cat, lounging beside her on the striped chaise longue. “You’d have adored her.”

The venue for the annual ball had been a strange one, certainly not an obvious place for a celebration. Willemstad’s Tula Hall was a former church on a cliff overlooking the sea, with a frontage of a grave face that had spat out its tombstones of teeth to the four winds, so that the paths to its aluminiumbattened door formed broken sixes and eights out of respect for those buried before. But Elizabeth arranged for a hundred lanterns to be hung about the grounds and its look of horror became, somehow, one of mischievous magic.

The preparations for this auspicious occasion would keep Elizabeth busy for months. Details of the place servings, menus, guest lists, would find her waking in the night to the dirge of her grinding teeth, and instantly she would think of Tula Hall and its sinister visage. Nothing, not even the wet eyes of her husband’s admiration at the end of another annual success, was worth it but for that chance to lock arms with Candy Brompton, knowing the ball was happening around them and they could raise a little merry hell.

“I’ve known three personal saviours in my life,” Elizabeth said, stroking the cat’s head with rough love. “Two were called Leopold and the other would have to be Candy. She’d arrive the day before the ball from her Washington residence and stay on for two days afterwards to give her body chance to recover. Her drink: whiskey-sours. Pink champagne. Really she was the antidote to the tight-fisted stress that would otherwise have crippled me. The first thing Candy did on arrival was hug me till I felt I’d be cracked between her breasts like a nut shell. Then she’d retrieve a jeroboam of champagne from her bag and insist we take the edge off with a glass downed in one, followed by,” and here Elizabeth lowered her voice, her eyes twinkling with pleasurable guilt, “a single line of the purest cocaine you could find. ‘Savour that snort,’” Candy would say, “‘That’s what diplomatic privilege tastes like.’”

Elizabeth stopped, appalled, for the cat was staring at her with admonition. “It was just the one line a year,” she said, deciding against revealing the single line before coffee but after dessert that Candy reasoned would set them up for another hour of sober conversation, or the one after midnight that would ensure they matched the hardened revellers with an unflagging, bright-eyed enthusiasm, or the restorative 4am line that was ‘for victory and for luck.’ But they were the extent of her annual naughtiness and, somehow, she could hide it all, drunk, so very drunk but able to keep everyone smiling while she enjoyed one true feeling of joy, the one that came with Candy and her mischief.

Candy rated the arses of the waiters from an eye-popping A to a saggy, zaggy Zee, and got the Prime Minister’s wife to join in. She spiralled on the dancefloor with a Bedlam abandon, her big hair a red beacon that suggested she was on fire. She mineswept the drinks left at the empty tables then went in for the night’s kill. And when Elizabeth skipped an hour to get her Leopold, made amorous by cocktails, satisfied and snoring, his dinner jacket still on and his satin shinies around his ankles, she would slip back to the hall to await Candy’s return from the rendezvous with whichever waiter had been awarded an A for ass-stounding that year.

“It didn’t matter to me that Candy was married,” Elizabeth explained to the cat. “Oh yes, she was married to a senator, the sleeping senator we called him because he would always retire before midnight. It was clear, you see, Candy could belong to no one, quite the opposite of me tugging on Leopold, leaning on him and then, one day, him tugging, leaning back. Besides, Candy’s husband was a tyrant! A bully with a permafrost heart! A predilection, Candy insisted, for the same waiters she desired. I didn’t feel alone, the traces of my madness the only company, when Candy was in my vision. Instead, I was part of a sorority of two, ‘blood sisters,’ I suggested once but Candy shook her head and insisted we were blood stars. ‘Just the two of us in the expanse of a Man’s sky,’ she said.” Elizabeth remembered her finishing another whiskey sour and proffering her glass to the waiter who had become a lover to find himself a waiter again. ‘Thank heaven for you, Elizabeth,’ she had said. ‘Free of judgement even for this ageing lady of the night. We will be remembered.’
