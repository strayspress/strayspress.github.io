A Bicycle Ride Through Amsterdam

From Chapter Fifteen of Dreamers Are Welcome

She awoke after an unexpected sleep with still an hour and a half to spare before her rendezvous with a friend so, after a fast cup of coffee, she said goodbye to Leopold, who was back on the steps examining the skies, and started out on her bike towards the market at Albert Cuypstraat. By nature of being human it was not the photos she had closely examined but the one that she had not dared view that stayed with her. Riding down the wide highway that was Reguliersgracht she had seen only one face in the crowds outside the ethnic bakeries; it appeared and was repeated throughout the journey and made her legs circle with more conviction, as if she could only escape it with greater speed.

In the market she bought kilo bags of ground almonds and chose the best oranges, dates, lemons and limes, tucking them all into her bike basket as if it was a mixing bowl. Still one universal face turned to her from behind the market stalls; it appeared on white faces, on brown, over the make-up of women and the beards of men. “This does not happen,” she whispered to herself, and she tried to start a conversation with a boy, no more than sixteen, who was supervising a stall that sold the same T-shirt in 20 colours, but he was smoking hashish and looked through her.

“I would like a little,” Granda said. She held out a hand and he looked at it as if it was a mass of alien tentacles but he passed it to her with a chortle that sounded just like Mr van Erbel reading a newspaper over breakfast. She had good etiquette for a communal smoker (what had the best times of life been, outside the bedroom, but a consideration of life while sitting in a circle of three, or five, or eight, having a joint passed to you before puffing and passing it on?) and took only a few deep drags before returning it. She felt her bones become soft and uncrackable, as if she was now a sponge, soaking in the smell of macaroons and blackcurrant jam, the taste of almonds and sugar on the tip of her tongue. But the noise from the market, the chatter of customers, the shouts of a man insisting on Good on Earth, the trundling of a cart being pulled to a stall that was packing up early, it all sounded garbled as if all the dykes of the country had cracked and splintered and let in an enervating sea that hid any face, regardless of how many times it was replicated. “The old lady’s a gangster,” the faceless boy behind the stall said, she could make out the words in their individual bubbles, and she stuck up a thumb at him and got back on her bicycle.

She rode away with a conviction that she could out-pace any ghost and with it any feeling of unpleasantness. Her pedalling was frantic but still denied her any victory in childish races with other bikes; younger people passed by her although they appeared to be cycling at leisure. A middle-aged woman with sandy hair and a maroon sweater knit chunky and chaotic seemed to take great pleasure in passing her, flashing a smile of derision. In pursuing her with all her might round a corner Granda drifted onto a tram line, only to be snapped back into the present by hissing, screeching smoke and abuse shouted along oceanic currents.

Granda did not care and to demonstrate this she stuck up two fingers and yodelled back a graphic description of a painful way for any man to die. The tram driver was under water and she hoped the message would carry but, what did it matter, she was back on her bike and she was thinking of her friend, this woman of perhaps 55, the woman of the window. Granda thought back to dressing herself today for the meeting and she thought of all the times she had prepared for a rendezvous, all the primping of skin, the preening of thin but soft hair into little urchin curls, the way she squeezed her under-developed breasts into a training bra that gave the impression of a semblance of cleavage, so she would smile when the man, always the man, she was to meet would look her in the eyes before checking out her bosom with his cheery hello. How lovely to be free of it all, she thought, the clenching need to attract the attention of men; the ache I felt when I was not touched for one week- the feeling of absence between my legs. I am living with myself the priority, finally. She understood, at last, and no man would breach the bubble of love for herself, and no one else was an equal to her, not even the cat and especially not the Madame.

She was cycling through the Red Light District and a sober engineer had pulled the plugs hidden below Dam Square for the water was subsiding and soon the sea was gone. She saw faces now that were only different, a swell of a hundred ethnic groups that betrayed sharp noses or lid-less eyes or furry skin or lithe, leopard cheekbones, and she recognised that people moved in packs just like animals and there was invariably a man at the front striding out with a camera instead of a spear, or sometimes a bottle of beer or sometimes a cigarette. She caught a whiff of marijuana being smoked and smiled as if she had recognised an old friend just by their smell in a darkened room, and looked out, trying to pinpoint the culprit. Instead, she stopped at the corner of a scarlet, cobbled street and got off her bike, looking into the window of a sex-shop that advertised dildos lined up like stalagmites; a red-lit display that could have been the entrance to the centre of the earth.

It was an eternally busy street she pushed her bike down. Its opposing sides were close together, sex-shops facing off against the habitual coffee stops and others that sold pipes and seeds and little pills that made you think you could fly. Two Chinese girls pointed in wonder at a window full of butt plugs, they shook with laughter although they hid their mouth with hands solid as steel. Other tourists seemed to be taking everything in their stride, shaven-headed men parading past as an unstoppable force, upturned dunce hats in their hands filled with frites and sauces of many colours.

Granda ignored it all, the ragga music from a dimly-lit bar, the songs of a vagrant slumped against a shop wall in a puddle of her piss. She passed underneath a flapping rainbow flag and listened to a few moments of soap opera from the men within, then looked at her watch. Perfectly on time. The café was just next door. Time to meet a friend, she thought with a sudden feeling of warmth, as if she was stepping gingerly over hot coals, and she propped her bike against the wall, alongside two others, but decided to carry the shopping from the basket in with her.

It was only when she was inside that she realised how the outside street had been overpowered by the odour of fried food for now she could only smell jasmine. It was a relief and she took off her coat before a red-haired woman with a nose piercing appeared from the kitchens and said, “Hallo again! Your friend is upstairs, waiting.”

More steps, Granda thought, but she remained silent apart from, “A small red wine, when there is time.”
